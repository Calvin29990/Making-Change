What does a currency move do to a debt that is not in your currency?

An infrastructure project earns in the local currency and often borrows in a hard currency. When the local currency loses value, the debt does not change — but the amount of local currency needed to pay it does. That gap is what this page lets you move, slowly, to see what it means.

Why the cost is zero here, and why that is the point. While the parity holds, nobody prices a break, so the hedge looks free. That is the honest reading of a pegged pair: cheap cover, thin liquidity, and a risk that is not in the price.

The trap to watch. Hedging everything at zero cost always looks perfect — that is why the cost is a visible field. A hedge that removes the shock but costs more than the shock is not protection, it is a transfer you chose to make in advance.

What this does not say. It does not say that hedging makes a project greener, nor that a financial cost is an environmental one. It says something narrower and more defensible: a currency shock consumes local cash that could otherwise service the same asset, so the cost of avoided carbon rises with the exchange rate. Whether that matters for climate outcomes is an empirical question this prototype cannot answer.

What this page is not

  • Not a forecast. No probability is attached to any shock.
  • Not a quote. The hedged rate and cost are typed by you, not priced by a market.
  • Not a full project model. Debt amortises in equal slices, cash available is constant, no taxes, no reserves, no covenants, no construction period.
  • Not causal. Comparing two scenarios shows a mechanism, not a proven effect of hedging on financing outcomes.

  • Not a market move. {{ s.local }}/{{ s.foreign }} is pegged: the shock above is a hypothetical break of parity.
